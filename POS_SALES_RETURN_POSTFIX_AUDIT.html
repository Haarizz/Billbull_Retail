Sales Return Ledger Review

BillBull Retail / ERP · branch feature/posclient · 1 October 2026 · read-only

Four of the five problems the first audit raised are now fixed in code, and the fixes agree with each other. What they also do is make the one unanswered question impossible to avoid any longer: a return now has two different effects on the customer's balance depending on which refund button the cashier presses — and nothing in the system decides which of those is correct when the original invoice was only partly paid.

01

What changed, and what it changed into

The four items the brief lists as fixed are in the code, and I verified each one rather than taking the fix list at face value. Three are complete. One — customer outstanding — is implemented correctly on the two surfaces it touches and absent from five others.

None of the fixes introduced a regression. The card/bank settlement reuses the voucher posting's shape, is duplicate-guarded on its own reference, and sits inside the approval transaction, so it cannot post without the return. The statement rows are read-only projections. The outstanding netting is a subtraction on a derived figure, not a write.

The structural consequence

Every refund method now clears the AR credit the return journal posts — except CUSTOMER_CREDIT, where leaving it is the point. So AR control and the customer sub-ledger now agree for the first time. That is the fix's real achievement, and it is also what makes the allocation question in §04 urgent: the two halves of the system are finally consistent with each other, and consistently answer a question nobody has decided.

04

The allocation question

This is the brief's §10 and §11, and the answer the code gives is: it depends entirely on the refund method, and the invoice is never involved. sales_invoices.balance is read and row-locked, never written, on every path.

Take the stated example — invoice 10,000, paid 6,000, outstanding 4,000, return 2,000:

Refund methodinvoice.balanceEffective outstandingStatement closingNet Cr to ARModel
CUSTOMER_CREDIT4,0002,0002,0002,000A — receivable reduced
CASH_REFUND4,0004,0004,0000B — money back, debt intact
CARD_REFUND4,0004,0004,0000B
BANK_TRANSFER4,0004,0004,0000B
CREDIT_VOUCHER4,0004,0004,0000C — voucher 2,000 held at GL 2061

So all four of the brief's models are present, selected by a dropdown at the till. There is no Model D: nothing splits the return across the unpaid and already-paid portions of the invoice, and nothing checks the invoice's payment state before allowing a cash payout. The cashier's choice of refund method is the accounting policy, and the policy is unwritten.

Where this bites

A credit customer with 4,000 outstanding returns 2,000 of goods and the cashier presses Cash Refund. The business hands over 2,000 in notes and still shows the customer owing 4,000 — on an invoice they have only paid 6,000 of. Net position: the business is out 2,000 of cash and 2,000 of goods against a 4,000 receivable it will still chase. Every ledger is internally consistent. The outcome is commercially wrong, and no code prevents it.

Return greater than outstanding (brief §11)

Invoice 10,000, paid 8,000, outstanding 2,000, return 5,000. The system does not recognise the question. It posts Cr AR 5,000 and settles 5,000 by whichever method was chosen, with no awareness that only 2,000 of it is an open receivable:

  • CUSTOMER_CREDIT — effective outstanding becomes 2,000 − 5,000 = −3,000. The subtraction at CustomerService:292 is not floored at zero, so the Customer List shows a negative balance and totalPaid = totalSales − outstanding overstates by 3,000. The statement closing balance goes to −3,000 too. Arithmetically consistent, but there is no "customer credit" concept to hold the 3,000 — it is just a negative receivable.
  • CASH_REFUND — 5,000 leaves the drawer. Outstanding stays 2,000. The customer is paid for goods they never paid for.
  • CREDIT_VOUCHER — a 5,000 bearer voucher is issued, outstanding stays 2,000, and the customer can redeem the voucher while still owing.

No guard exists at any layer. assertReturnableQuantitiesStillAvailable validates quantity against the invoice, never value against the balance.

Credit Voucher is a real instrument: a liability account, a balance, a redemption path, a printed barcode. Customer Credit is a reporting subtraction wearing an instrument's name. It reduces what the customer appears to owe, and there is no way to apply it to an invoice, cancel it, convert it to cash, or report on its outstanding pool. The only thing that consumes it is a future invoice the customer does not pay — the credit absorbs the new debt silently, with no allocation record and nothing on the invoice to show it happened.

Not a defect, a missing half

For a simple credit-account retailer, "the credit sits on the account and the next invoice nets against it" is a legitimate model, and that is what the code does. What is missing is any record of the netting. Accounting can see the pool shrink but cannot say which invoice consumed which credit — which is exactly the question an AR clerk asks when a customer disputes a statement.

Opening-balance handling

                + Σ invoice totals before startDate

Correct. Only ledger-resident credits are brought forward — a cash/card/bank/voucher return nets to zero within its own period and must not appear in an opening figure. The query (sumLedgerCreditBeforeDate) and the row-emitting predicate (SalesReturn.settlesOutsideReceivable()) are separate implementations of the same rule, and I checked them against each other across all four cases (refundMethod set, null with returnAction null, "Refund", "Credit Note") — they agree.

07

Reconciliation model

The identities the system should satisfy, and whether it does:

✓ GL 1100 net Cr from a return = statement net effect of that return
✓ GL 1100 net Cr from a return = change in effective outstanding (Customer List / detail)
✓ GL 2061 balance = Σ open credit_vouchers
✓ drawer DROP_OUT total = Σ CASH_REFUND return amounts for the session

✗ GL 1100 balance ≠ Σ sales_invoices.balance (reconcileAR; drifts by every CUSTOMER_CREDIT return)
✗ GL 1200 balance ≠ Σ stock_movements.unit_cost × qty (drifts by the COGS of every restocked return)
✗ AR aging total ≠ effective outstanding (aging reads invoice.balance)
✗ GL 1013 refund leg ∉ card_settlements (refunds never enter settlement reconciliation)

The first group is the fixes working. The second group is the same single root cause showing up in four places: the return credit lives in the GL and in a derived subtraction, and never in the stored figures that four other subsystems treat as the sub-ledger.

SubLedgerReconciliationService.reconcileAR compares GL 1100 against SalesInvoiceRepository.sumGlobalOutstandingBalance(). Neither side knows about returns, so a CUSTOMER_CREDIT return makes the GL side move and the sub-ledger side stay put. The difference grows monotonically. Nothing is wrong with either number — they are answering different questions, and the reconciliation assumes they are answering the same one.

Card is the weakest chain. Only GOOD restocks; the other four are scrap. That is coherent and createJournalFromSalesReturn now documents it explicitly — a scrap return posts the revenue/VAT/AR entry alone and deliberately omits the inventory entry, so the cost stays on the books as a loss. What no condition does is route the scrap anywhere: no quarantine location, no write-off to 7502, no supplier-claim record. The goods are physically in the store and in no system.

COGS resolution — improved since the baseline

resolveActualCogs now walks three sources in priority order:

  1. DeliveryNoteBatchConsumption rows — the exact batch/WAC cost at the original sale. Prevents WAC distortion.
  2. SalesInvoiceItem.cost — the cost-at-sale snapshot stamped at POS checkout. Survives a later change to the product master.
  3. ProductPricing.cost — current master cost, for legacy rows predating both snapshots.

And the guard is now a hard failure rather than a silent zero: a restockable return whose cost cannot be resolved is rejected at postJournalForApprovedReturn with the offending item codes named in the message. That is a real improvement — it converts a silent GL/inventory divergence into a blocked approval the cashier can act on.

Two risks confirmed still present

Inventory valuation diverges from GL 1200 on every restocked return Still broken

StockMovementService.reverseOutboundStock (line 426) never sets unitCost, so the inbound row is null-cost and sumGlobalInventoryValue() ignores it — while the GL debits 1200 by the resolved COGS. reconcileInventory drifts by that amount, against a 1.00 AED tolerance.

This remains a genuine trade-off rather than an oversight: the null is also what keeps the return from moving the weighted average. But the COGS figure is now resolved reliably from the DN consumption snapshot, which is exactly the number that would make stamping it safe. The trade-off that justified the null has weakened.

inventory_balances cannot see its own movement Still broken

InventoryBalanceService.refresh is still @Transactional(propagation = REQUIRES_NEW) (line 59) and is still called from refreshBalance inside the approval transaction. It recomputes from a ledger that does not yet contain the just-inserted movement, and the exception handler swallows failures. The cached balance lags by one return until the next movement for that product/warehouse refreshes it.

Reports reading the ledger are correct; reports reading inventory_balances show the stale figure. This is the brief's §15 question answered: yes, still present, in the same form.

Non-batch stock silently skipped when no DN resolves Still broken

resolveReturnWarehouseId (line 672) returns null unless the linked invoice has a linkedDeliveryNote whose DN carries a warehouse. On null, applyNonBatchStockReturns logs and continues — while the GL still debits Inventory, because the journal's restock test keys on itemStatus == "Good", not on whether a movement was actually posted.

That is the precise mechanism of the divergence: two independent predicates decide "did stock come back", and they can disagree. The code's own log line admits it. POS sales normally have a DN, so exposure is mostly back-office invoices.

  • Cash, card, bank and Customer Credit all land in refundTotal. A Customer Credit return — where no money moved at all — is reported as a refund paid out.
  • Credit Voucher is reported as a credit note.
  • exchangeTotal is permanently zero.
  • No cash-refund figure is published, although pos_cash_movements now holds exactly that number.

refund_method has been on the row since V78 and is backfilled for legacy rows. The comment at line 2704 still reads "SalesReturn has no payment-mode field today, so a cash refund total cannot be computed" and sets cashRefundsSupported = false. That statement is no longer true, and the flag it justifies suppresses a figure the cash-refund fix made available.

X vs Z scoping

X is session-scoped: buildSessionReturnsSummary filters the branch+date return set down to returns whose linkedInvoice belongs to this session's invoices. Z is branch+date scoped: buildReturnsSummary(branchId, date) takes everything. Deliberate and documented — a return is a post-sale event with no inherent session — but it means the sum of a day's X-Report returns does not equal the Z-Report's returns whenever a return references an invoice from an earlier session. That is correct behaviour producing a figure that looks like a discrepancy, which is worth stating in the report UI.

Idempotency and print safety

The brief asks specifically whether the new settlement posting introduced duplicate risk. It did not. Five independent guards now exist, and they compose:

So every retry path the brief lists is covered. The row lock serialises concurrent confirmations; a retry arriving after commit is rejected by the APPROVED guard; and if a journal-level retry ever did get through, each findDuplicate returns the existing entry rather than posting a second. The cash guard deliberately ignores voided movements, so a voided-then-reissued refund is a legitimate new payout rather than a blocked duplicate — correct, and worth keeping in mind when reading drawer history.

Print cannot touch money

Verified for the brief's §24. handlePrintReceipt and the auto-print effect call only getPosPrinters → printSalesReturnReceipt → runPrint. The only backend writes on that path are the PosPrintJob create/dispatch/report records, which are best-effort audit bookkeeping. There is no call to saveSalesReturn, updateSalesReturnStatus, any stock, GL, voucher or refund endpoint on any print or reprint path.

  • autoPrintedRef is keyed on returnNumber and set before printing, so a printer failure surfaces as a toast rather than a retry loop.
  • printedReturnNumber is set only on a truthy runPrint result, so a failed print leaves the button as "Print", not "Reprint".
  • A CREDIT_VOUCHER return waits on voucherLoading before printing, so the paper names the real voucher.
  • Auto-print is gated on entryPoint === POS; the back-office register keeps the manual button.

One approval remains one financial transaction, one stock effect, one settlement. Printing is downstream and inert.

CustomerService:292 — invOutstanding.add(opnOutstanding).subtract(returnCredit). A return credit exceeding the open balance yields a negative outstanding, which then propagates into totalPaid = totalSales − outstanding (line 310), overstating lifetime payments by the overshoot. Whether to floor it or to surface the negative as customer credit is the §15 decision, not a code choice.

Card refunds never enter settlement reconciliation New

The fix posts Cr 1013 Merchant Clearing but creates no payments row and no CardSettlement linkage. 1013 is the account the settlement subpackage exists to reconcile against the acquirer's statement, and the refund leg is invisible to it. The GL is right; the reconciliation that depends on the GL cannot close.

This is not a regression — before the fix there was no entry at all. It is the next step of the same work, and the brief's §05 traceability chain is where it surfaces.

Stale comment contradicts shipped capability New

PosSessionService:2704 still asserts that SalesReturn has no payment-mode field and sets cashRefundsSupported = false. V78 added refund_method, the cash-refund fix created the drawer movements, and the comment is what currently justifies withholding a cash-refund figure the data supports. Minor as code; it is the reason a correct number is not published.

15

Open decisions

The economic question, in BillBull's own terms

A return reverses a sale. The sale created two things: a receivable, and cash or a card receipt that partly or wholly settled it. Reversing the sale means undoing both, in proportion to what each one actually was.

returnAmount = unpaidPortion + paidPortion unpaidPortion = min(returnAmount, invoice.balance) → cancel receivable paidPortion = returnAmount − unpaidPortion → give money or credit back

Against BillBull's architecture that maps cleanly onto the five-row settlement table in §02, because the structure is already there:

  • The return journal already posts Cr 1100 for the full amount. No change.
  • The unpaidPortion needs a receivable reduction the system can see: an allocation against sales_invoices.balance, or a durable customer-credit record. Today the Cr on 1100 is the only trace, and five surfaces cannot see it.
  • The paidPortion needs the settlement leg already implemented — cash, card, bank or voucher. No change.

The architecture is right. What is missing is that nothing computes the split, and nothing records the receivable half anywhere other than the GL. That is one decision and one mechanism, not a redesign.

Decisions code cannot make

  1. Does a return against an unpaid balance reduce the receivable, and how is that recorded? Three options, with different aging behaviour: allocate against invoice.balance (aging corrects itself, but mutates a closed document); a standalone customer-credit record with its own allocation rows (aging needs a new input, and allocation becomes auditable); keep the derived subtraction and extend it to the five missing surfaces (cheapest, still has no allocation trail). This is the decision everything else waits on.
  2. Should the system compute the unpaid/paid split, or keep the method as the policy? If it computes it, a return against a partly paid invoice would offer the cashier only the eligible settlement amount, and the 2,000-cash-on-a-4,000-debt case becomes impossible. If the method stays the policy, that case needs an explicit warning or an authorization gate instead.
  3. May a return exceed the open balance, and what holds the excess? Today it may, and the excess becomes a negative receivable. Floor at zero, block, or introduce a real credit balance.
  4. Is Customer Credit a real instrument? If yes, it needs a balance, a redemption path, an allocation record, cancellation, and a report. If no, remove it from the vocabulary and let Credit Voucher cover the case — the voucher already does everything Customer Credit claims to.
  5. What should a card refund produce besides a journal? A refund payments row, a CardSettlement linkage, both, or an explicit decision that merchant clearing refunds are reconciled manually.
  6. Which date owns a return? return_date, trading_date, or the session's business date. One answer, applied to the document, its journals, its stock movement and its drawer payout.
  7. Should POS Net Sales be net of returns, and on which base? The back-office reports already net on a VAT-inclusive base; matching them is the obvious answer and needs confirming, since pos_day_close.net_sales is persisted history.
  8. Are cross-branch returns permitted? If yes, which branch owns the reversal — the selling branch or the receiving one?
  9. Where do scrapped returns go? A quarantine location, a write-off to 7502, a supplier claim, or nowhere by policy.
  10. Should the inbound movement carry a unit cost? Stamping the resolved COGS reconciles inventory with the GL and reintroduces WAC movement on returns. The improved COGS resolution makes this more attractive than it was.
  11. Should unlinked returns be possible? They are today, and they skip the quantity guard entirely.
  12. Should Replacement / exchange exist? X and Z have buckets for it and nothing ever fills them.

Implementation order

Sequenced so that nothing has to be rewritten by a later step, and so the decisions that need sign-off do not block the work that does not.

Now — no decision needed, no behaviour change

  1. Reject status: APPROVED on POST /api/sales/returns. Two lines, closes R7 and removes a phantom-return vector.
  2. Audit every approval, not just supervisor-gated ones. PosAuditService.logReturnInitiated and RETURN_APPROVED already exist with no callers.
  3. Fix the stale comment and javadoc at PosSessionService:2704 and createJournalFromSalesReturn. Both currently mis-state the system to the next reader.
  4. Quantify the historical residues in §13 as read-only queries. Needed to scope anything else, and it changes nothing.

Next — mechanical, independently valuable

  1. Bucket X/Z returns on refund_method instead of returnAction, and publish the real cash-refund figure. Closes R10 and stops reporting Customer Credit as a refund paid out.
  2. Make the stock-posted test and the journal's restock test one predicate, so GL 1200 cannot be debited for goods that never moved. Closes the R4 mechanism without deciding the warehouse-resolution question.
  3. Move InventoryBalanceService.refresh out of REQUIRES_NEW, or defer it to after-commit. Closes R12.
  4. Floor effective outstanding at zero, or surface the negative deliberately — whichever decision 3 gives.

After decision 1 — the receivable mechanism

  1. Implement the chosen mechanism for the unpaid portion.
  2. Extend it to all seven surfaces in one shared helper: credit limit, AR aging, reconcileAR, AR reports, alongside the three already done. One definition of outstanding, used everywhere.
  3. Then, and only then, the historical reconciliation JVs — the target state has to exist before old rows can be brought to it.

After the remaining decisions

  1. Unpaid/paid split computation and the return-above-outstanding guard (decisions 2, 3).
  2. Customer Credit as a real instrument, or its removal (decision 4).
  3. Card refund settlement linkage (decision 5).
  4. Single business date across all legs, using trading_date (decision 6).
  5. POS Net Sales net of returns (decision 7) — note this changes a persisted figure, so it needs a stated effective date.
  6. Cross-branch policy, scrap routing, unit cost on inbound movements, exchange document (decisions 8–12).
One thing to protect

The five-row settlement table in §02 is the best thing about this module: every refund method posts the identical return journal and differs only in which account faces AR. Every item above fits inside that shape. Anything that proposes a different return journal per refund method is going the wrong way.
